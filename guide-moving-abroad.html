MOVING ABROAD

Making friends after moving abroad

A realistic plan for building a social life when the language, culture and routines are new.

By Nadiia Honda · Updated 1 September 2026 · 13 minute read

Moving abroad changes more than your address. Familiar jokes, social cues and support networks disappear at once. Building friendships in this situation works best when you combine patience with a small repeatable routine.

Many people expect that a new country will automatically deliver a new circle of friends. In practice, the first months are often quieter than expected. This is normal. The goal is not to force instant closeness, but to create conditions where real connection can grow.

Build three kinds of connection

Do not expect one person to meet every need. Look for three layers:

  • Practical support — someone who understands the details of your new city (bureaucracy, transport, neighbourhoods).
  • Shared interest — someone who enjoys the same activity or topic.
  • Emotional honesty — someone with whom you can speak more openly about how the move actually feels.

These relationships may grow at different speeds. One person can eventually fill more than one role, but starting with multiple paths is more reliable.

Use newcomer spaces — but do not stay only there

International meetups and language exchanges are useful because everyone expects introductions. They reduce the awkwardness of being the only new person in the room.

At the same time, staying only in the “expat bubble” can limit how much you feel part of everyday local life. Add one local recurring activity as well: a neighbourhood workshop, sports club, volunteer shift, library group or community class. That creates a bridge into ordinary routines.

Learn the local rhythm

Notice when people make plans, how far in advance they schedule and whether invitations are usually direct or casual. Some cultures prefer clear yes/no answers; others use softer language. You do not need to imitate every custom. Understanding the rhythm simply helps you avoid reading a cultural difference as personal rejection.

Keep the next step small, specific and easy for both people to decline.

Create a weekly anchor

Choose one place or activity you visit at the same time each week. Repetition turns strangers into familiar faces. Say hello, remember one detail and ask a follow-up question the next time. Friendship is often built from remembered details rather than impressive conversations.

A weekly language table, running group, market visit or volunteer shift can become that anchor.

When the language feels limiting

Use simple sentences and be open about learning. Most people respond well to honesty and effort. Suggest activities that do not require constant talking: walking, cooking, a museum visit, board games or a photo walk. Shared activity lowers pressure and creates natural topics.

You can also prepare a few useful phrases in advance: how to introduce yourself, how to ask for recommendations, and how to suggest a short follow-up plan.

A two-week starter plan

Week one: Attend one newcomer event and choose one recurring local activity. Focus on showing up and learning names.

Week two: Return to the local activity, message one person you met and propose a specific short plan. Keep the invitation easy to accept: coffee after class or a thirty-minute walk.

After these two weeks, keep the strongest option and give it at least four meetings before deciding it does not work.

Common challenges and how to handle them

  • Feeling invisible: Focus on one or two settings where faces become familiar.
  • Language fatigue: Balance conversation-heavy activities with quieter shared ones.
  • Homesickness: Keep contact with old friends while still investing locally.
  • Comparing timelines: Other people’s “I made friends in a month” stories are not a standard.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it usually take?

Many people start to feel a basic sense of social belonging between three and nine months if they keep a consistent routine. Deeper friendships often take longer. Consistency matters more than speed.

Should I focus only on other internationals?

They are a useful starting point, but mixing in at least one local recurring activity helps you feel more rooted in the city itself.

What if I am still learning the language?

Choose some activities that rely more on doing than talking, and be transparent about your level. Most people appreciate the effort.
