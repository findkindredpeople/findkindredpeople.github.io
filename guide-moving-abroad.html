MOVING ABROAD

Making friends after moving abroad

A realistic plan for building a social life when the language, culture and routines are new.

Written and reviewed by Nadiia Honda · Published 1 September 2026 · 13 minute read

Moving abroad changes more than your address. Familiar jokes, social cues and support networks disappear at once. Building friendships in this situation works best when you combine patience with a small repeatable routine.

Many people expect that a new country will automatically deliver a new circle of friends. In practice, the first months are often quieter than expected. This is normal. The goal is not to force instant closeness, but to create conditions where real connection can grow.

Build three kinds of connection

Do not expect one person to meet every need. Look for three layers:

  • Practical support — someone who understands the details of your new city (bureaucracy, transport, neighbourhoods).
  • Shared interest — someone who enjoys the same activity or topic.
  • Emotional honesty — someone with whom you can speak more openly about how the move actually feels.

These relationships may grow at different speeds. One person can eventually fill more than one role, but starting with multiple paths is more reliable.

Use newcomer spaces — but do not stay only there

International meetups and language exchanges are useful because everyone expects introductions. They reduce the awkwardness of being the only new person in the room.

At the same time, staying only in the “expat bubble” can limit how much you feel part of everyday local life. Add one local recurring activity as well: a neighbourhood workshop, sports club, volunteer shift, library group or community class. That creates a bridge into ordinary routines.

Learn the local rhythm

Notice when people make plans, how far in advance they schedule and whether invitations are usually direct or casual. Some cultures prefer clear yes/no answers; others use softer language. You do not need to imitate every custom. Understanding the rhythm simply helps you avoid reading a cultural difference as personal rejection.

Keep the next step small, specific and easy for both people to decline.
