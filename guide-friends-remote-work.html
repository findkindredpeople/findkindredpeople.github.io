REMOTE WORK

How remote workers can make local friends

Build a local routine that survives your working hours. Start with the time you can protect, then choose a setting where conversation is welcome.

Published by Nadiia Honda · Prepared with AI assistance · Updated 11 October 2026 · 5 minutes read

Working remotely can remove a familiar source of contact without giving you a replacement. A busy online workday can still end with nobody nearby to see. Before buying a coworking pass or joining several groups, identify which part of your week actually permits contact: a lunch break, an evening without calls or a predictable day off.

Match the activity to your working pattern

Use your ordinary week rather than an unusually quiet week as the starting point. Write down the earliest reliable finishing time, the total minutes available before your next responsibility and whether that slot repeats.

A social plan for three remote-work patterns
Working patternA possible formatQuestion before committing
Fixed local hoursAn evening class or hosted library discussion.Can I finish work, eat and travel before the start, including preparation?
Calls across time zonesA daytime activity on a genuinely free break or a weekend session.Will I have to leave to take a call? Is attendance for the full session expected?
Rotating hours or on-call workA drop-in setting with clearly stated joining rules.Must I book each date? What happens if I need to cancel?

A quiet library work session is a different format from a hosted social activity. Follow the library's work and conversation rules. Use breaks or organised events for conversation; headphones, focused work and a brief greeting are reasons to leave someone space.

Count the whole visit, including preparation

Write the calculation before choosing: activity + outward and return travel + preparation + a buffer. Add the visit fee, materials and transport separately. An unstated fee is a question to ask, rather than evidence that an event is free.

Worked example: the travel and preparation figures below are fictional assumptions. Session lengths come from the linked Berlin organisers. Imagine having 120 minutes available after work, with a 30-minute return journey.

The calculation can rule out an attractive option. That saves a rushed visit and a cancellation later. The activity comparison keeps missing information visible and counts travel in your own time limit.

Choose one anchor you can return to

An anchor is a specific activity at a workable time, rather than a promise to socialise more. Choose something you would enjoy even if conversation is brief. Check whether the programme is a fixed course, an open recurring meeting or a one-off event: these have different expectations about returning.

  • For a course, ask about the complete fee, remaining places and whether late entry is allowed.
  • For a drop-in group, confirm the next date, newcomer contact and whether leaving early is acceptable.
  • For volunteering, ask about induction, minimum commitment, expenses and a responsible contact. Offer time you can reliably provide.

In Germany, the local-group finder points to adult education, libraries and volunteer agencies. A directory finds providers; their current programmes determine what you can join.

Write a boundary that makes attendance possible

Put the activity and travel into your calendar. If necessary, reserve an earlier work stopping time and discuss availability with your team through the normal work process. Do not promise a host that you will attend while planning to keep a client call running in the background.

A proposal to adapt

“I enjoyed today's discussion. I'm planning to come to the next session if the date works. Would you like to get a short coffee afterwards? I'll need to leave by [time].”

Use this only after contact feels welcome. The other person can prefer the group activity without wanting coffee. A coworking desk, class place or volunteer role does not include an obligation to make friends.

Review feasibility before adding more

After a visit, record one practical answer: Did the journey fit? Could you follow the activity? Was conversation part of the format? Would you return even without an invitation? If your schedule failed, change the time or distance first. If the format felt wrong, try a different activity. If you simply enjoyed a quiet hour among people, that can also be a reason to keep it.

Your next step is to find one recurring programme, fill in the full time calculation and resolve its largest unknown. You can use the seven-day planner for preparation and follow-up without filling every evening.
