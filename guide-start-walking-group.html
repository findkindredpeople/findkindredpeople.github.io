A REPEATABLE MEETUP

How to start a friendly walking group

A walk gives conversation a purpose, allows comfortable pauses and costs almost nothing. The best group begins with a simple promise people can understand and keep.

Written and reviewed by Nadiia Honda · Published 9 September 2026 · 7 minutes read

Starting a social group can sound like a large responsibility. A walking group is different: it needs no complicated program, expensive venue or expert host. People arrive, follow a known route and talk at their own pace. That structure can feel especially comfortable for newcomers, remote workers and anyone who dislikes formal networking.

Define one clear purpose

Choose a description that tells people what to expect: “A relaxed Saturday walk for adults new to the area” is clearer than “Come make friends.” Decide whether the pace is gentle or brisk, whether children or dogs are welcome, and which language will mainly be used. Clarity helps the right people say yes.

Avoid promising that everyone will become close friends. Your job is to create a respectful opportunity for repeated contact. Friendship remains voluntary and develops differently for each person.

Plan the first route carefully

Use a public, well-travelled route you have already walked. For the first event, aim for 45–60 minutes with an obvious start and finish. Check lighting, road crossings, accessibility, toilets, public transport and a place to stop if the weather changes.

Publish the meeting point, start time, approximate distance, pace and finish time. Uncertainty creates more anxiety than a simple plan.

Begin small

Invite three to eight people. A small group is easier to coordinate and gives everyone a chance to speak. You can begin with neighbours, classmates, colleagues or people from an established local online group. Do not add strangers to a private chat without permission.

Use a simple invitation: “I’m starting a relaxed one-hour walk on Sunday at 11:00, meeting outside the library. We will stay on public paths and finish at the same place. Would you like the details?”

Create a welcoming start

Arrive ten minutes early. Greet each person, introduce newcomers and repeat the practical plan. A helpful opening is: “We will walk for about an hour. Feel free to change conversation partners, take a quiet moment or leave early if needed.” This removes pressure to perform socially.

Use names, but do not force personal introductions. Instead of asking everyone to explain why they are lonely or new, offer a neutral prompt: “What is one place nearby you would recommend?”

Help conversations move naturally

People often stay beside the first person they meet. Pause once or twice at a safe point and gently mix the group: “Let’s make sure everybody gets to talk with someone new on the way back.” Watch for anyone repeatedly walking alone and include them without drawing public attention to it.

Silence is not failure. Walking allows people to look ahead rather than maintain constant eye contact. The shared activity carries the moment until another topic appears.

Set boundaries before problems arise

  • Keep first meetings in public places and daylight.
  • Do not publish participant photos without explicit consent.
  • Do not share phone numbers or personal details for other people.
  • State that harassment, pressure and discriminatory behavior are not accepted.
  • Encourage participants to arrange their own transport.

If someone ignores boundaries, address the behavior clearly. Protecting the group is more important than avoiding one uncomfortable conversation.

Choose a sustainable rhythm

A walk that happens every two weeks is better than an ambitious weekly plan that disappears after a month. Use the same meeting point and approximate time when possible. Send one reminder the day before and a brief confirmation if weather changes the plan.

You do not need to carry every event alone. After several walks, invite a reliable participant to lead occasionally. Share only the responsibilities they willingly accept.

After the walk

Send a short message: “Thank you for joining today. Our next walk is planned for 23 September at 11:00. Suggestions for an accessible route are welcome.” Do not pressure absent members to explain themselves. Make it easy to return after missing a week.

A first-event checklist

  1. Walk the route yourself.
  2. Write a clear invitation and group boundary.
  3. Confirm the weather and meeting point.
  4. Arrive early and welcome people individually.
  5. Finish at the promised time.
  6. Share the next date only if you can maintain it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need permission?

A casual small walk on public paths usually needs no special organization, but local rules differ. Larger events, commercial promotion or use of protected areas may require permission. Check the relevant local authority when unsure.

What if only one person comes?

One person is enough for a useful walk. Thank them, follow the planned route and treat the event as the beginning of consistency rather than a failed launch.

Should the group use WhatsApp?

Only with consent. Explain that participants’ phone numbers may become visible to other members. An email list or privacy-conscious platform may be more suitable for people who prefer not to share a number.
