LANGUAGE EXCHANGE

How to turn a language exchange into a real friendship

Balance practice, curiosity and boundaries so the connection can grow beyond small talk.

Published by Nadiia Honda · Published 1 September 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026 · Reading time

Agree the purpose before the session

Ask what each person wants to practise and how long you can stay. A language exchange is an agreement to help each other practise; friendship is a possible development, not something either person owes.

Choose a public place and confirm any venue rules, cost and booking requirements. Ask whether corrections should happen during speaking or after a thought is finished.

Worked example: A one-hour exchange

Fictional example: Mira wants to practise German and Robin wants to practise English. They both have an hour. They agree the following structure, including time to review the next meeting:

MinutesPurposeWhat to agree
0–5Choose the topic and correction style.Each person says what they want to practise.
5–30First language.Let the learner finish a thought; correct in the agreed way.
30–55Second language.Switch at the agreed time so the second person also practises.
55–60Review and decide what comes next.Discuss another date only if both want one.

This is one possible structure. Reduce the time or change the split by mutual agreement; do not assume fluency levels or language preferences from someone’s nationality.

Send a clear proposal

Would you like to practise at [public place] on [day/time] for an hour? We could do 25 minutes in [language 1], 25 in [language 2], and use the remaining time to choose a topic and review. Would you prefer corrections while speaking or afterwards?

Illustrative wording. Replace brackets, remove anything untrue and send only if you want to make this invitation.

When the exchange becomes uneven

If one language takes the whole meeting, refer to the agreement: “We planned to switch now. Shall we use the remaining time for [language]?” If the session ends before your practice time repeatedly, ask whether a different structure would work. You can stop the arrangement if it does not meet your needs.

If levels differ, choose simpler topics, short written prompts or a slower pace. Neither person needs to become a free teacher, translate private documents or accept personal questions they do not want.

Suggest an activity only when you want one

If the conversation feels mutual, ask about a short public activity connected to an interest you discussed. For example: “You mentioned that exhibition. Would you like to visit together sometime? We could check the dates and price first.”

A decline can simply mean the person wants language practice only. You can continue a useful exchange on those terms or decide it is not what you are looking for. There is no fixed number of sessions after which an invitation is required.

Before confirming

Check the date, public venue, cost, time in each language and correction preference. The preferences tool can help discuss setting and pace. For an existing plan that keeps slipping, use the scheduling guide.
