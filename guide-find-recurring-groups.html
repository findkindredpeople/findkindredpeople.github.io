RECURRING GROUPS

How to find groups where friendship can actually grow

One-off events create introductions. Recurring groups create familiarity, trust and realistic opportunities to meet again.

Written and reviewed by Nadiia Honda · Published 1 September 2026 · 11 minute read

When you are new in a city, a large calendar of events can feel productive without producing real connection. The useful question is not “Where can I meet the most people?” It is “Where will I see some of the same people again?” Repetition removes the pressure to turn one conversation into an instant friendship.

Start with an activity you would do anyway

Write down three activities that are enjoyable even if you speak to nobody: walking, drawing, reading, climbing, cooking, volunteering or practicing a language. This protects your energy and gives you a natural reason to return. Avoid choosing only by popularity; the best group is one you can attend consistently.

Look for evidence of repetition

Prefer groups with a clear schedule, a named organizer and recent updates. Weekly or twice-monthly meetings are usually easier for relationship-building than annual festivals. Read the description carefully: beginner-friendly, small-group and structured activities reduce uncertainty.

If the group has photographs, check whether the setting matches your comfort level without trying to identify participants.

Test three options, then narrow

Choose one interest group, one neighbourhood activity and one service-oriented option such as volunteering. Attend each once. Afterward, rate travel time, cost, atmosphere, accessibility and whether returning feels realistic. Keep the best one or two for four meetings before deciding they are not working.

Consistency matters more than charisma. Being recognizable is the first stage of belonging.

Make the second visit easier

On the first visit, learn one name and one practical detail: what someone is working on, which route they walk or how long they have attended. On the next visit, say hello and ask one natural follow-up. Remembering a small detail communicates attention without demanding intimacy.

Notice healthy group signals

Good organizers explain expectations, welcome newcomers without forcing participation and respond clearly to safety questions. Be cautious if a group pressures you to share private information, pay unexpectedly, recruit others, ignore boundaries or move immediately to a private location.
