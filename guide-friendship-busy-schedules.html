TIME AND FRIENDSHIP

Making time for friendship with a busy schedule

Friendship does not require unlimited availability. It needs a rhythm both people can realistically keep.

Published by Nadiia Honda · Published 1 September 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026 · Reading time

Start with the time you actually have

Choose one person and one realistic window. Count travel, the activity itself and any fixed obligation afterwards. A free hour is not a one-hour meeting if the journey takes twenty minutes each way. Ask how much notice the other person needs; a shift worker and a parent may have different planning constraints.

A shorter plan only helps if both people want it. Leave room to decline, suggest another format or meet less often.

Worked example: Two calendars, one possible plan

Fictional example: Alex finishes work late and has a 40-minute break on Fridays. Noor can meet for an hour on Wednesday evenings but has childcare duties afterwards. Their usual dinner plan keeps being cancelled.

OptionWhat needs checkingDecision
Wednesday dinnerAlex cannot arrive before Noor needs to leave.Do not keep offering this slot.
Friday coffee near Alex’s workplaceTravel must fit Noor’s available time; the café must be open.Ask about one date. It is still a proposal.
Ten-minute call at another agreed timeBoth people need a quiet enough moment and must want a call.Use this if no in-person option fits.

The useful result is an option both can confirm. A calendar entry or an unanswered invitation is not a shared commitment.

Offer a small menu, then choose one

  • Ten minutes: a brief call or a short check-in message.
  • Thirty to sixty minutes: a public coffee, lunch or walk, with travel counted separately.
  • A longer occasion: an exhibition or other shared activity booked only after checking cost and availability.

Start with the smallest option that suits the situation. When a plan is agreed, confirm the public place, start and finish time and how you will handle a cancellation.

If you have to cancel

I am sorry I cannot make our coffee on [date]. I know you set aside time. I could do [alternative 1] or [alternative 2] instead. Would either work for you?

Illustrative wording. Replace brackets, remove anything untrue and send only if you want to make this invitation.

Offer alternatives only when you can genuinely keep them. If your capacity has changed for a longer period, say so: “Weekly plans are difficult for me this month. Would one short meeting later in the month suit you?”

If neither option works

Ask the other person to suggest a workable window or a different format. If no overlap exists, you can agree to occasional contact without pretending a regular meeting has been arranged. Avoid sending a new invitation every day.

Slow replies alone do not tell you why someone is unavailable. Consider whether they engage in other ways, offer alternatives or attend agreed plans. The guide to one-sided effort helps separate observations from assumptions.

Your next step

Write down one available window, the travel it requires and one smaller alternative. Use the friendship preferences tool to discuss pace and setting, or Social Dice for a step within today’s available time.
