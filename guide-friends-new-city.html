How to Make Friends in a New City: Choose Your Next Step

Start with the obstacle you actually have: finding a place, returning to it, starting a conversation or making a second plan.

Published by Kindred · AI-assisted content · Published 1 September 2026 · Updated 20 September 2026 · Reading guide

After moving, “meet more people” is too broad to put in a calendar. A more useful first task is to identify where the process stops for you. If you have not found a suitable group, another message template will not fix the journey. If you already meet people every week, joining five more groups may not help you follow up.

These are choices, not stages you must complete on a deadline. If a place feels unwelcoming or unsafe, you can stop without earning a certain number of visits.

2. Build a shortlist you can actually use

Choose one interest you would enjoy even if nobody exchanged numbers: walking, making something, reading, a sport, language practice or a local volunteering role. Search for the activity with your district and words such as “weekly”, “beginners”, “open session” or “new members”. Read the organiser’s own listing rather than relying on a date shown in a search result.

For each of two options, record the next session, total time away from home, first-visit cost and how to join. Include compulsory materials or membership payments; a “free” activity can still require a ticket for the journey. If a price is missing, ask instead of treating it as zero.

Use the numbers you found. The activity comparison tool adds session and return travel time, compares full visit costs and keeps missing information visible.

For example, a 60-minute discussion with a 20-minute return journey needs 80 minutes. A 90-minute workshop with 40 minutes of travel needs 130 minutes. With only 90 minutes free, the second option does not fit even if its description is more exciting. This is an illustrative calculation, not a real event listing.

When a listing leaves questions unanswered

Hello, I am interested in [activity] on [date]. Can someone new join that session? Could you confirm the finish time, the full first-visit cost and whether I need to book? I would also like to check [language or access need].

If there is no answer before you would need to travel, choose another option or wait for confirmation. You do not need to pay or disclose personal details to prove you are serious about friendship.

3. Make the first visit small and specific

Before leaving, check the location, return journey and any booking confirmation. Choose a manageable finish time. At the session, you can begin with the host: “It is my first time here. Is there a place I should start?” That gives you a practical role without needing to introduce yourself to the whole room.

Ask one question related to the activity, listen to the answer and offer a small detail of your own. If the other person is concentrating or gives a short answer, leave them space. See the English and German conversation examples for joining, asking for clarification and ending politely.

There is no requirement to leave with someone’s number. An equally useful result is knowing who organises the session, when it happens again and whether being there felt manageable.

4. Decide whether to return, adjust or move on

  • Return: you liked the activity, the arrangements worked and you would be comfortable attending again. Save the next confirmed date.
  • Adjust: the activity was promising but one obstacle mattered. Ask about a quieter session, beginner level, shorter visit or different time.
  • Move on: the group ignored your boundaries, the cost was unclear, the journey was not workable or you simply disliked the activity. Use what you learned to change one shortlist criterion.

If a conversation felt mutual, ask about a small shared next step: “I am planning to return on Thursday. Would you like to talk for ten minutes afterwards?” Check the actual date and their interest before treating this as an arrangement. The follow-up guide explains how to respond to yes, no, uncertainty or silence.

When local choice is limited

In a small town or with restricted transport, start with the nearest repeatable option, an occasional activity in the next town or a moderated online group around a specific interest. A monthly visit you can keep may be more realistic than a weekly journey you cannot. Online contact still needs an agreed time, boundaries and a setting you trust.

Keep a record of the next action

Write down one thing to do, when you will do it and what information you need first. “Ask the library about the next discussion on Tuesday” is actionable. “Become more social” leaves the decision for later. Use the seven-day planner to turn your chosen step into a checklist with room to rest.
