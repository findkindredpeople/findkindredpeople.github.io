MAKE FRIENDS

How to make friends in a new city without forcing it

A practical, low-pressure plan for building real friendships after moving. Based on what actually works for newcomers.

By Nadiia Honda · Updated 1 September 2026 · 14 minute read

Friendship after a move rarely begins with one perfect event. It usually grows from repeated, ordinary contact: the same café, the same class, the same Saturday walk. The goal is not to impress everyone. It is to create enough safe repetition for familiarity to become trust.

Most advice about “making friends” is either too vague (“just put yourself out there”) or too aggressive. This guide focuses on what actually works when you are tired, a little lonely and still learning the city.

Start with two real interests (not ten)

Choose activities you would still enjoy if you met nobody. A language exchange, running group, volunteer shift, book club, drawing class, board-game night or cooking workshop all work well. The activity itself removes pressure and gives every conversation a natural subject.

Avoid the trap of joining five different groups at once. Two recurring activities are usually enough. More than that often leads to surface-level contact and quick burnout.

Use the four-week rule

Attend one recurring activity for at least four weeks before deciding it does not work. At the first visit, people may already know each other. By the third or fourth visit, your face is familiar and conversation becomes easier. Many newcomers leave after one or two sessions and never give the group a fair chance.

A simple first-week plan

  1. Pick one recurring small-group activity that matches a real interest.
  2. Visit one neighbourhood place (café, park, library) twice at roughly the same time.
  3. Ask one person a specific local question (“Which bakery do you actually like?”).
  4. Send one concrete follow-up invitation within 48 hours if the conversation felt mutual.

Move from chat to friendship

Replace “we should meet sometime” with a small, specific proposal: “I am going to the Sunday market at eleven. Would you like to join for half an hour?” Specific plans are easier to accept and easier to decline without awkwardness.

Keep the first invitations short and low-pressure. Coffee walks, a quick museum visit or a shared activity you both already like work better than long dinners or parties.

Aim for consistency, not instant closeness. A dependable acquaintance is often the beginning of a real friend.

When it feels slow

Do not treat a quiet week as a verdict on your social worth. Moving interrupts routines, language confidence and energy. Keep the plan small enough to repeat. Two promising contacts are more valuable than twenty conversations you never continue.

Loneliness after a move is extremely common. It does not mean you are doing something wrong. It means your previous social structure disappeared and a new one has not yet formed.

Practical timeline most people experience

  • Weeks 1–3: Mostly observation and short conversations. Focus on showing up.
  • Weeks 4–8: First real follow-ups and small shared plans.
  • Months 3–6: A few people start to feel familiar and reliable.
  • Months 6–12: Deeper friendships begin to form if both sides keep investing.

These numbers are averages, not rules. Some people find a close circle faster. Others need longer. Consistency matters more than speed.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Joining too many groups and burning out within a month.
  • Expecting deep friendship after one or two meetings.
  • Only talking about how hard it is to make friends (it becomes self-fulfilling).
  • Giving up after the first awkward conversation.
  • Waiting for the “perfect” person or group instead of starting with good-enough options.

How to keep the momentum

Once you have two or three promising contacts, protect the relationship with small, regular contact. A short message after a meeting, a shared photo from the walk, or an invitation to the next event keeps the connection alive without pressure.

If someone does not respond or repeatedly cancels, gently move your energy elsewhere. Friendship requires mutual effort.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it usually take?

There is no fixed timeline. Most people notice the first real sense of belonging between three and nine months if they keep showing up consistently. Repeated contact and mutual effort matter more than speed.

Should I use friendship apps?

They can help, especially when paired with a clear activity and a public first meeting. Protect personal details until trust develops. Treat apps as a supplement, not the main strategy.

What if I am introverted or shy?

Choose smaller groups and activities that have a clear structure (classes, workshops, volunteer shifts). Prepare one or two simple questions in advance. Give yourself permission to leave after 60–90 minutes if your energy is low.

Is it harder after 30?

Yes, because schedules are fuller and many people already have established circles. The same principles still work: recurring activities, specific invitations and patience. Many strong adult friendships start later in life.
