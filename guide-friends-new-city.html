How to make friends in a new city: 10 practical steps

Making friends after moving usually works better through repeated contact than through one perfect event. Here is a realistic way to build a social circle without forcing instant closeness.

Written and reviewed by Nadiia Honda · Published 1 September 2026 · Updated 17 September 2026 · 6 minutes read

Moving to a new city can reset your social life overnight. You may have work, housing and daily routines under control while still having nobody to call on a free evening. That gap is common because friendship depends on familiarity, and familiarity takes repetition.

If you are wondering how to make friends in a new city, focus less on meeting as many people as possible and more on creating places where you can see the same people again.

The fastest-feeling strategy is often the slowest-looking one: go back to the same places and people often enough for familiarity to grow.

1. Choose one or two recurring activities

Classes, volunteering, sports, language exchanges, hobby groups and community events work well when the same people return. Pick activities you would still enjoy if you did not make a friend immediately.

Need ideas? See how to find recurring groups in a new city.

2. Become a regular somewhere local

A café, library, park, gym or market can become a social anchor simply because you return. Repeated casual contact makes a place feel less anonymous and creates more natural openings for conversation.

3. Start with easy local questions

You do not need an impressive opening line. Ask something connected to the place: “Have you been to this class before?”, “Do you know another good place nearby?” or “Which event here is worth trying?”

For more examples, read what to say when you know nobody.

4. Return before you judge the group

The first visit often feels the hardest because everyone else looks more familiar with the setting. Give a promising recurring activity several visits before deciding that you do not fit in.

5. Follow up while the interaction is still fresh

If a conversation felt mutual, make a small specific suggestion instead of saying “we should meet sometime.” For example: “I am coming back next Thursday. Want to get coffee afterward?”

6. Keep early invitations short

Coffee, a walk, a market visit or a shared activity can feel easier than a long dinner or open-ended evening. Short plans reduce pressure and make a second meeting more likely if you both enjoyed the first one.

7. Build more than one connection

Do not put all your social hopes into the first friendly person you meet. A healthier approach is to build several light connections and allow the strongest ones to deepen naturally.

8. Turn acquaintances into friends with repetition

Friendship usually grows through a sequence of ordinary interactions: see each other again, remember something from the previous conversation, make a small plan, follow through, repeat.

For this stage, see how to turn an acquaintance into a friend.

9. Create the gathering you wish existed

If you cannot find the right group, invite a few people to something simple: coffee and a walk, a board-game hour or a weekend market visit. You do not need to become an event organizer.

Use the small meetup checklist to keep it easy and safe.

10. Protect your energy and boundaries

You do not need to say yes to every invitation. Choose a social rhythm you can repeat. If you are meeting someone you first knew online, use public places and independent transport. See how to meet someone online safely.

A simple first-week plan

  1. Choose one recurring activity.
  2. Visit one local place twice.
  3. Start one short conversation.
  4. Save one second activity for next week rather than filling every evening.
  5. If one conversation feels mutual, make one specific invitation.

What if making friends feels very slow?

Do not use one quiet week as evidence that nothing is working. Look for smaller signs of progress: recognizing people, receiving a reply, knowing where you want to go on Saturday, or having one place where you no longer feel completely new.

If the emotional part of moving is harder than expected, read how to overcome loneliness after moving.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Joining too many groups and burning out.
  • Expecting close friendship after one meeting.
  • Waiting for other people to make every plan.
  • Taking every declined invitation personally.
  • Staying only in one-off events where you never see the same people again.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make friends after moving to a new city?

Choose one or two recurring activities, return to the same local places, start small conversations, follow up with specific invitations and give new connections repeated chances to develop.

Where can adults meet new friends in a new city?

Recurring settings usually work better than one-off events. Examples include classes, volunteering, hobby groups, sports, language exchanges, community events and regular local meetups.

What if I am shy or introverted?

Choose smaller structured activities, prepare one or two easy questions, stay for a manageable amount of time and focus on repeated contact rather than meeting many people at once.

Should I use friendship apps?

They can help, especially when you move from chat to a clear public activity. Treat them as one way to create repeated real-world contact rather than the whole strategy.
