What to say when you know nobody

Conversation starters and follow-up techniques that feel natural for newcomers and shy people.

By Nadiia Honda · Updated 1 September 2026 · 12 minute read

A useful conversation opener does not need to be clever. It needs to fit the shared situation and give the other person an easy way to answer. Most people are not waiting for a brilliant line. They are waiting for a low-pressure invitation to talk.

Begin with what is already around you

Ask how someone found the event, what they recommend to a newcomer, or whether they have tried the activity before. Shared-context questions feel natural because neither person has to reveal anything private right away.

Openers that usually work

  • “What brought you to this group?”
  • “Have you been here before?”
  • “What would you recommend to someone new to the city?”
  • “I am choosing my next weekend walk. Do you have a favourite area?”
  • “How did you find this place?”
  • “Is this your first time trying this activity?”

Listen for a thread

Good conversation is less about asking many questions and more about noticing one detail. If someone mentions photography, ask what they like to photograph. If they mention a difficult move, acknowledge the feeling before changing topics.

People feel heard when you pick up on something they already said, not when you move through a list of prepared questions.

Share, do not interview

After asking a question, add a small piece of your own experience. “I am new too; I have been trying one neighbourhood each weekend.” This creates balance and gives the other person material to respond to.

A simple structure that works well:

  1. Ask a light, context-based question.
  2. Listen and pick one detail.
  3. Respond with a short related observation of your own.
  4. Leave space for them to continue or change the subject.

End without awkwardness

You can leave warmly: “I am going to get another drink, but I am glad we spoke.” If the conversation felt mutual, offer a specific follow-up rather than requesting private contact information immediately.

Respect short answers. Interest should feel mutual, not extracted. Some people are simply not in a talkative mood, and that is fine.

Prepare two questions before arriving and give yourself a modest goal: speak with one person for five minutes. Confidence often arrives after action, not before it.

If you are nervous

Almost everyone feels some nervousness in new social situations. The difference is that experienced people have learned to act anyway. A few practical ways to reduce the pressure:

  • Arrive a little early so the room is quieter.
  • Choose a seat near the edge rather than the centre.
  • Have one or two “safe” topics ready (the event itself, the weather, a local recommendation).
  • Give yourself permission to leave after a reasonable time.

Common mistakes

  • Rapid personal questions that feel like an interview.
  • Complaining about how hard it is to meet people (it can close the conversation).
  • Debates designed to test the other person.
  • Pressure to exchange contact details too quickly.
  • Talking only about yourself without checking whether the other person is still engaged.

Turning a short chat into a possible friendship

If the conversation felt easy, you can end with a light invitation that does not demand a big commitment:

  • “I’m coming to the next session. Maybe I’ll see you there.”
  • “There’s a market near here on Sundays. I’m planning to go if you ever want a low-key walk.”

Specificity helps. Vague “we should hang out sometime” almost never turns into a real plan.

Frequently asked questions

What if they give very short answers?

It may mean they are shy, tired, or simply not interested in a longer conversation. You can try one more gentle question and then move on without taking it personally.

How do I know when to ask for contact details?

Wait until there has been some mutual exchange and the conversation has lasted more than a few minutes. Even then, a soft approach works better: “Would it be okay if I messaged you about the next event?”

What if English (or the local language) is not my first language?

Most people are patient when they see effort. Simple, clear sentences are better than complicated ones. You can also mention that you are still learning — many people will adjust their pace.
